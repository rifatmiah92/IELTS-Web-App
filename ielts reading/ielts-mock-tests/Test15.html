Bristlecone Pines

The earth's oldest living inhabitants and secrets of extreme longevity

A On the dry, windswept mountain tops of the Great Basin in the western United States, at altitudes of over 3,000 m, grow the bristlecone pines (Pinus longaeva, Pinus aristata). The bristlecone has adjusted to places that no other tree wants to inhabit. What makes bristlecone pines truly remarkable is their longevity: their average age is over 1,000 years, with the oldest specimen — Methuselah — dated at 4,723 years. The extreme age was discovered in the 1950s by Edmund Schulman, who worked under A. E. Douglass at the Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research at the University of Arizona.

B Douglass had noted that wide rings were produced during wet years and narrow rings during dry seasons. This ring-to-ring variation is referred to as sensitivity. Patterns of ring variability allow precise dating of climatic conditions in the past. When ring sequences match between overlapping samples, chronologies can be extended thousands of years into the past.

C Schulman learned that trees growing in upper-forest zones under stressful conditions showed sensitive drought records. Surveying bristlecones from California to Colorado with assistant C. W. Ferguson, they found the oldest trees at elevations of 3,048 to 3,354 m on dolomite soils where no other plant life could survive. The trees growing in the most extreme conditions with scant soil and moisture were the oldest.

D Bristlecone needles live twenty to thirty years; thus, adding new foliage requires very little energy, sustaining photosynthetic capacity over decades of severe stress. When damaged by weather or fire, living bark dies back gradually, allowing trees to survive on a thin strip of living tissue (such as Pine Alpha surviving 4,000 years on a 25 cm strip of bark). Furthermore, highly resinous wood prevents invasion from bacteria, fungi, or insects.

Environmental factors also protect the trees: dry subalpine air preserves them from rotting. Dolomite soil and extreme exposure mean considerable space between trees with virtually no leaf litter or ground cover, preventing lightning fires from spreading from tree to tree. To protect Methuselah from human vandalism, it is not marked or identified with any plaque.
