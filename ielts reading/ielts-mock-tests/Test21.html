The Art of Deception: The Truth About Lying

Do tiny changes of facial expression reveal whether someone is lying?

1 Forty years ago, research psychologist Dr Paul Ekman was asked by young psychiatrists in training whether there was any way to be sure a patient is telling the truth. Filming 12-minute interviews, Ekman slowed a tape down after a patient confessed to lying. Across just two frames lasting less than 1/15th of a second, he caught an intense expression of extreme anguish. He termed this 'micro-expression': rapid, involuntary displays of emotion that the subject intends to conceal.

2 Over four decades, Ekman validated Charles Darwin's proposition that expressions of rage, disgust, contempt, fear, surprise, happiness, and sadness are universal across human cultures from the US to Papua New Guinea. Micro-expressions are virtually impossible to suppress when lying. While only 50 out of 15,000 tested people ('naturals') spotted them untrained, Ekman showed almost anyone can be trained. The FBI, CIA, and legal professionals have routinely called on him for training.

3 Ekman's research inspired the television drama series Lie to Me, starring the fictional Dr Cal Lightman. Initially apprehensive that the show might foster false beliefs among jurors and lead to miscarriages of justice, Ekman consulted on each script to ensure 80–90% accuracy. Ekman defines a 'true lie' as requiring deliberate intent to deceive without notification, focusing strictly on high-stakes deception.
