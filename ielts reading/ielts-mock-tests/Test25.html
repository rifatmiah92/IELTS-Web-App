Lights Along a Dangerous Coast

How the Stevenson dynasty engineered Scotland's iconic rock lighthouses

A Until the late eighteenth century, sailing around the coast of Scotland was among the most dangerous undertakings in northern Europe. Reefs lay hidden just below the surface, tides ran with great force, and storms could arrive within hours. Some headlands carried coal fires lit to warn ships away, but these were unreliable: they depended on the goodwill of local landowners, failed in bad weather, and were easily mistaken for one another. In 1786, Parliament established the Northern Lighthouse Board to provide proper navigation lights. Its first engineer, Thomas Smith, had previously been a maker of lamps rather than a builder of towers, and it was his stepson and assistant, Robert Stevenson, who would eventually take on the most ambitious projects.

B The greatest of these was the Bell Rock, a reef some eighteen kilometres off the east coast, which lies beneath the sea at high tide and had long been a graveyard for ships. Earlier proposals to build on it had been abandoned as impractical, and it was only after a series of disastrous storms that the Board finally approved the plan. Work began in 1807, but the builders could labour on the reef for only a few hours each day, when the tide fell, and at first they lived on a vessel anchored nearby. On one occasion, the boats that carried them drifted away, leaving the men stranded on the rock as the water rose, and disaster was avoided only when a passing pilot vessel took them aboard. The celebrated engineer John Rennie had been consulted on the project and later claimed much of the credit, although most historians now regard Stevenson as the true designer.

C The tower that rose from the reef owed a good deal to an earlier lighthouse, John Smeaton's Eddystone in England. Like Smeaton, Stevenson had the granite blocks cut with dovetail joints, so that each stone locked into its neighbours, allowing the structure to withstand waves that would have shattered ordinary masonry. To reduce the danger of the labourers being cut off by the sea, the builders eventually erected a wooden barrack on stilts above the reef, where they slept. The finished tower, thirty-five metres high, was first lit in 1811. Its lamp rotated, showing red and white beams in turn, so that sailors could tell at once which light they were seeing.

D Optical technology advanced rapidly in the following decades. In 1822 the French physicist Augustin Fresnel designed a lens made of concentric rings of glass that gathered the light from a lamp and projected it as a single powerful beam, requiring far less fuel than the polished metal reflectors then in use. Robert's son Alan, who succeeded his father as the Board's engineer, travelled to France to inspect the invention and persuaded his employers to adopt it. His most remarkable achievement was Skerryvore, a forty-two-metre tower built on a lonely reef off the west coast and completed in 1844. Its granite had to be shipped from the island of Mull, and the project took years of dangerous work. Poor health forced Alan to retire in 1853, and his brothers David and Thomas took over.

E The family's influence extended beyond lighthouses. Thomas Stevenson, a keen student of weather, designed a louvred wooden box for housing thermometers outdoors; the 'Stevenson screen' is still used by meteorological stations around the world. His only son, Robert Louis, was expected to join the family firm and began studying engineering at Edinburgh. He soon lost interest, switched to law, and then abandoned that too in order to write. The author of Treasure Island and Kidnapped nonetheless acknowledged his debt to the family trade, and the voyages he made with his father to inspect lights supplied settings and atmosphere for some of his early work.

For those who staffed the lights, life was demanding and often lonely. Keepers on rock stations such as the Bell Rock might spend weeks cut off from the mainland, with supplies delivered by boat only when the weather allowed. Each day followed a strict routine: trimming wicks, polishing brass and lenses, winding the clockwork that turned the lamp, and recording the weather in a log. Because a light had to burn every night, stations normally had three keepers, so that one could rest or fall ill without leaving the lamp unattended. In 1900, all three keepers disappeared from the Flannan Isles lighthouse in the Outer Hebrides, a mystery that has inspired poems, novels and a film. Most investigators believe a huge wave swept them away, but nothing has ever been proved.

G Electric lamps arrived in the twentieth century, followed by radio beacons and, eventually, satellite navigation, which allowed ships to fix their position within metres. Automation made keepers unnecessary, and the last staffed lighthouse in Scotland, on Fair Isle, was converted in 1998. Yet the lights have not been switched off. Many ships still rely on them as a back-up when electronic systems fail, and the Bell Rock continues to shine, controlled remotely from Edinburgh. Some former keepers' cottages have been turned into holiday homes, while the Stevensons' notebooks and drawings are preserved as a record of a family that lit a dangerous coast for several generations.
