New Zealand home textile crafts of the 1930s to 1950s

Objects made by previous generations of women should be valued more highly

1 Domestic crafts do not build monuments to their makers, nor do they create wealth, and too often they have not survived. In consequence, the domestic crafts produced by mothers, grandmothers, and great-grandmothers in New Zealand are often overlooked. But needlework should be preserved, for it helps to convey a sense of people's identity and past beliefs in these remote islands in the Pacific Ocean.

2 Women of previous generations expected to sew; they had to dress their families in a time when few people could afford to buy what they were capable of making. For some women this work may have been unwelcome drudgery, but for others it was an opportunity to explore their identities, beliefs, and dreams with the only means readily at hand—a needle, some thread, and a piece of fabric.

3 Textiles are frustrating to collect. They are fragile; they stain; they develop rust marks; they fray and rot. If they are used, they deteriorate; yet if they are merely stored, the owner does not fully enjoy possession. That is probably why they have not been as popular to collect as antique furniture or fine china, which are more durable investments. Domestic crafts are also seldom signed, as creators never expected them to be seen outside the family home.

4 It does not help that such work falls into the category of crafts rather than fine arts, because crafts are looked on too often as a poor cousin to true creativity. If things are made to be useful within the home by ordinary housewives, society has agreed they cannot be art. The collector of textiles is like an archaeologist, digging through junk shops to understand history through physical evidence.

5 The domestic crafts of 1930–1950 cannot be separated from women's magazines showing photos of how prosperous people lived. Magazines provided inducements to social betterment, alongside printed patterns available at low cost for everyday items like aprons, tea cosies, and curtains. Collectors today find greater enjoyment in individual, imaginative needlework where flaws and serendipity take on a life of their own.

6 Nostalgia reminds us of the care with which women selected fabrics. Old patchwork quilts in particular represent an unknown family's compressed history: pyjamas, ball gowns, school dresses, all thrown together as a wish for something better during hard economic times.
